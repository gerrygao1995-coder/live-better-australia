# 22. School age and teenagers

Build health skills gradually, listen to young people and coordinate support with schools and clinicians. Privacy, consent and services vary with age and circumstances.

## AU-0379 · Make school-night sleep a shared plan

Agree on a realistic wind-down time, a regular waking time and a place for devices to charge. Ask about stress, discomfort or snoring if sleep remains difficult, rather than treating every late night as a discipline problem. Seek advice when poor sleep affects learning, mood or daily life.

| --- | --- |
| Cost / effort | A$0 for routine changes; assessment costs vary. |
| Potential value | A workable sleep routine and earlier help for persistent problems. |
| Applies to | School-age children and teenagers. |
| Basis | Official / professional guidance |

## AU-0380 · Address school distress before absences accumulate

Notice persistent changes in mood, attendance, friendships or schoolwork and ask what feels hard. Involve a trusted school staff member and a health professional when distress is continuing. Make a specific next contact rather than assuming the child will arrange everything alone.

## AU-0381 · Keep a factual record of bullying

Write down what happened, when it happened and who was involved; preserve relevant messages without circulating them. Contact the school's nominated staff member and agree on a safety response and review date. Listen to the young person's preferences and avoid making them responsible for fixing the bullying alone.

## AU-0382 · Keep conversations possible during conflict

Choose a calm moment to listen before trying to solve a disagreement. Set clear, consistent limits around safety while allowing choices about less important matters. Revisit a conversation after everyone has settled; persistent aggression, distress or major changes deserve professional support.

## AU-0383 · Prepare a discreet period kit

Offer a choice of suitable period products, spare underwear and a small bag for school or outings. Explain how the chosen products are used and changed, and check what supplies the school provides. Let comfort and practical needs guide the choice rather than assuming the most expensive or reusable option suits everyone.

| --- | --- |
| Cost / effort | Low ongoing A$ cost; reusable products cost more upfront. |
| Potential value | Less disruption when a period starts unexpectedly. |
| Applies to | Young people who menstruate and their carers. |
| Basis | Practical suggestion |
| Editorial review | 2026-10-09 |

**Read the source:** [RCH: Periods](https://www.rch.org.au/kidsinfo/fact_sheets/Periods/)

## AU-0384 · Get disruptive period symptoms assessed

Record period dates, bleeding concerns and effects on school, sleep or sport. Arrange medical advice when pain, heavy bleeding or changing symptoms disrupt everyday life. A record can help the conversation, but do not delay care while trying to collect a perfect pattern.

## AU-0385 · Give school the current allergy plan

Arrange a clinician-developed allergy or anaphylaxis plan and share the current version with the school and other carers. Confirm where prescribed emergency medicine is kept, who can access it and how excursions are handled. Follow the plan and call 000 for anaphylaxis; paperwork must never delay emergency treatment.

| Basis | Official / professional guidance |
| Editorial review | 2026-10-09 |

<a id="au-0386"></a>

## AU-0386 · Practise allergy-safe independence

Teach a child with food allergy to check labels, tell food providers about their allergy and avoid swapping food. Before parties or overnight visits, speak with the responsible adult and agree on food and medicine arrangements. Gradually practise these skills with the young person instead of relying only on adults remembering.

| --- | --- |
| Cost / effort | Usually A$0; suitable food may cost more. |
| Potential value | More confident allergy management away from home. |
| Applies to | Children with food allergy and their families. |
| Basis | Official / professional guidance |
| Editorial review | 2026-10-09 |

**Read the source:** [RCH: Food allergies](https://www.rch.org.au/kidsinfo/fact_sheets/Food_allergies/)

## AU-0387 · Treat a suspected concussion seriously

Stop activity after a suspected concussion and arrange medical assessment; do not send the child back into play that day. Call 000 for severe warning signs such as unconsciousness, a seizure or worsening difficulty staying awake. Follow the clinician's observation instructions even if the child initially seems better.

| Cost / effort | Assessment costs and time away from activity vary. |

## AU-0388 · Coordinate the return to learning

After a concussion, ask the treating clinician about a gradual return to school and any temporary changes to workload, breaks or screen tasks. Tell the school what symptoms to watch for and when the plan should be reviewed. Recovery varies; do not use another child's timetable as a target.

## AU-0389 · Get clearance before returning to contact sport

Use the treating clinician's staged return-to-sport plan and the sport's current concussion rules. Keep training progress separate from feeling eager to compete, and arrange review if symptoms return. A helmet does not make an early return safe, and same-day return after suspected concussion is inappropriate.

## AU-0390 · Use a fitted mouthguard for contact sport

Ask a dentist about an appropriate mouthguard for the child's sport, teeth and any braces. Check the fit as the child grows and replace a damaged or poorly fitting guard. Store it clean and bring it to training as well as matches.

## AU-0391 · Offer practical support to quit vaping

Ask about vaping without shaming the young person, including when cravings or peer pressure are hardest. A GP, pharmacist or Quitline can help choose age-appropriate support. Keep devices and liquids away from younger children, and seek emergency help for serious breathing problems or suspected poisoning.

## AU-0392 · Ask how confidentiality works

Before a youth health appointment, ask what stays private, when information may need to be shared and how family members are involved. Discuss practical privacy issues such as appointment messages and shared Medicare arrangements with the service. Exceptions apply, especially where safety is at risk; do not promise absolute secrecy.

## AU-0393 · Investigate persistent learning difficulties

Talk with the teacher about the specific tasks causing difficulty and which supports have already been tried. Ask a GP or appropriately qualified professional about assessment where difficulties persist. Request evidence for any paid intervention and agree on goals and a review point before committing to a long package.

## AU-0394 · Protect activities beyond academic performance

Keep some regular time for friendships, enjoyable activity and rest alongside school commitments. Ask the young person which activities help them feel connected and which have become burdensome. Persistent withdrawal, distress or loss of interest needs a conversation and potentially professional support, rather than simply a busier timetable.

## AU-0395 · Explain puberty without comparison

Use clear, age-appropriate language and make room for private questions about changing bodies and emotions. Avoid comparing a young person's development with siblings or classmates. Offer a trusted clinician or youth service when they want a different person to speak with, or when changes are causing concern.

## AU-0396 · Practise making a health appointment

Help the young person choose a suitable service, find its contact details and prepare the first question they want answered. Let them make as much of the booking as they feel ready for, with support available. Check local headspace availability, fees and referral requirements rather than assuming every centre provides every service.

| Potential value | Growing confidence in navigating health care independently. |
| Applies to | Teenagers and young adults, with support if wanted. |
